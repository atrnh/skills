# Teaching principles

These principles help learners understand ideas and practice using them. Apply the sections that match the work. Planning a lesson depends most on its progression, and adapting it for slides depends most on keeping reasoning in view, but a single lab or lecture usually needs several. When general writing or engineering guidance conflicts with them, favor what helps learners understand and practice.

## Make the progression meaningful

A learning sequence should develop understanding. Each part should have a reason to follow the previous one and a purpose in the work ahead.

- Start with the learner's current knowledge and the questions the task is likely to raise.
- Give each section a clear contribution to the learning goal. Related topics alone do not establish a useful order.
- Order sections so that each one's prerequisites have already been taught.
- Make transitions explain the relationship between parts. Reorder the content when a transition cannot supply a missing logical connection.
- Read the sequence again as a learner and address unanswered questions that would block understanding or practice.

Learner questions help shape the sequence; they do not replace learning objectives or require answering every possible question. Deliberate exploration can leave questions open when resolving them is part of the learning experience.

## Connect examples to concepts

An example should help learners recognize and use an idea beyond that example. Make the relationship between the concrete details and the underlying concept explicit.

- Establish what the example is meant to explain and why that matters.
- Introduce the categories or criteria an example depends on before asking learners to interpret it.
- Connect the example's choices and results to the concepts that explain them.
- Preserve useful terminology when making an explanation more concrete. Learners need language they can carry into another situation.
- Include the reasoning that makes the lesson transferable, rather than leaving learners with only a pattern to copy.
- Demonstrate where observing the behavior teaches more than explanation alone. Explain every idea the lesson needs, even those you do not demonstrate.
- Keep a demonstration focused enough that learners can see the relevant cause and result. Reuse a running example for continuity without forcing every topic to fit it.

An example can come first when it gives learners a useful question or something to investigate. Supply enough context for that purpose, then connect the observation to the concept. Neither abstract vocabulary alone nor an unexplained example provides that connection. A complete demonstration fits when performing the whole process is the learning goal; otherwise, judge its scope by the understanding it produces.

## Match language to teaching purpose

Choose the sentence's language by what the passage should help the learner understand or do.

- Explain when building understanding. State what happens, why it matters, and how ideas connect.
- Connect action to reasoning when demonstrating. Make the purpose and result of each step visible.
- Direct when guiding practice. Make the learner's next action clear.
- Match the immediate passage. One learning experience can move between explanation, demonstration, and practice.

Purpose governs the language. Softening a command does not turn it into an explanation, and an explanation alone may leave a learner unsure what to do. Supply the understanding or direction the moment requires.

## Protect practice time

Spend the learner's effort on the judgment or skill the activity is meant to develop. Remove obstacles that consume time without contributing to that learning.

- Identify which decisions learners need to make for themselves.
- Supply starting points or examples when inventing them is incidental to the objective.
- Remove requirements whose learning value does not justify their cost within the available time.
- Make expected outcomes and deliverables concrete, especially when many valid solutions are possible.
- Preserve room for learner choice without making an open-ended task depend on guessing what counts as success.

Difficulty is useful when it belongs to the target skill. Do not supply the very reasoning learners need to practice, remove necessary safety checks, or simplify away conditions they must learn to handle. Reduce incidental work while preserving the intended challenge.

## Put essential reasoning in view

An explanation can exist in the source and still be missing from the learner's experience. When turning content into slides, presenter notes, handouts, or another presentation format, preserve the premises and connections needed to follow the lesson in the form learners will encounter.

- Check what learners actually see and receive. Confirm that each idea's premises appear before it in that form.
- Keep related ideas together and use meaningful shifts to guide presentation breaks.
- Use supporting material for elaboration without hiding the connection that makes the main content understandable.
- Judge pacing and fit in the presented result. Source structure alone cannot show whether an idea has been split awkwardly or lost.

The presented material need not reproduce every spoken explanation or supporting detail. Keep the essential relationship visible and use narration or notes for depth. When space or accessibility requires a break, preserve enough continuity for learners to follow it.
