# Contusion

Dorothy heard the carer's key at nine and had her cardigan on by the time the girl got to the kitchen. Long sleeves. She'd buttoned it to the neck.

"Morning, Mrs. Hale. You're up early."

"I am always up early. You are late."

"Bus," said the girl — Priya, from the agency, the third one since spring — and put her bag on the table and her clipboard on top of it. The clipboard had a form with boxes. Dorothy had read the form upside down on the first day. *Falls since last visit: Y/N.*

Priya made the tea too weak, as she always did. Dorothy told her so, as she always did. "Your tea tastes of the idea of tea."

"I'll do it stronger tomorrow."

"You said that on Friday."

She had fallen at about two. Getting up for the bathroom, the rug by the bed; she'd known the rug was a hazard for a year and kept it because her husband had bought it in Fez. She'd lain on the floor for what the clock said was forty minutes, looking at the underneath of the bed, and then she had got herself onto her knees and up by the bedpost one inch at a time, and she had not called anyone, because she knew what came after a Y.

Her left arm was purple from wrist to elbow. It throbbed in time with the kettle.

"Can I do your blood pressure?"

"If you must."

"I'll need your sleeve up."

"The other arm."

Priya looked at her. She had very steady eyes for someone who couldn't make tea. "It's usually the left."

"Today it is the right."

The girl did the right. She wrote the number down. Then she put the cuff away and sat back and did not pick up the clipboard, and the two of them listened to the fridge.

"My nan fell," Priya said. "She didn't tell anyone for a week. By the time they found out it'd gone to her hip."

"I'm sure that's very sad for your nan."

"It was, yeah."

Dorothy looked at the calendar by the back door. She'd been crossing off days since the last carer had asked her, in a tone she hadn't cared for, whether she'd thought about *options*. Two hundred and eleven. Every one of them managed.

The girl still hadn't picked up the clipboard. She was going to let her keep it. Dorothy could see that: the girl was going to write N and drink her own terrible tea and go on to her next old woman, and that would be the end of it, and then one night it would be the hip.

Dorothy unbuttoned her cuff and pushed the sleeve up to the elbow.

Priya didn't say anything. She didn't need to; her face did it for her.

"Well, go on," Dorothy said. "Write it down. And it's *contusion*, with an i-o-n. If you're going to put me in a home you can at least spell it."
