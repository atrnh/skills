# The Salt House

## I

The ferry put Nell ashore at four, with the tide going out and the light already thinning. She had a key, a list from the estate agent, and a roll of bin bags her mother had insisted on, as though the island had no bins.

The house stood where it always had, back from the shingle with its shoulders up against the wind. Someone had cleared the gutters. Someone had not cleared the step, where eleven winters of salt had crusted into a pale rind that cracked under her boots.

Inside it smelled of damp wool and paraffin. The calendar in the kitchen still said March. Not *the* March, she told herself, just a March; her mother had lived here another nine years after Rafe, and had simply stopped turning pages at some point, the way she'd stopped answering the phone after six.

Nell started with the kitchen because it was the easiest room to be unkind to. Tins. Jars of screws. A drawer of rubber bands that had fused into one brown knot. She filled four bags before she let herself look out the window at the water, grey and flat and entirely innocent, the way it had looked the morning they found the mooring empty.

He was nineteen. He took the boat out before anyone was up, and the boat didn't come back, and neither did he. The coastguard searched for three days. After that it was just the sea, and the sea doesn't give you anything back to bury.

## II

The shed was the job she'd been saving. Her father's tools, her mother's broken chairs, the lobster pots nobody had used since the eighties. She took the torch and the last of the bin bags and dragged the door open across the gravel.

The boat was inside.

She stood with the torch on it for a long time. The same blue hull, the name — *Kittiwake* — painted by Rafe in letters that leaned to the right. Upturned on two trestles, oars stowed underneath, the way their father had taught them. Dry. Dusty. Whole.

She put her hand on the keel as if it might be a different boat if she touched it.

"They towed it back," said Mr. Aske, when she went next door with the torch still in her hand. He was ninety, or near it, and he looked at her as if she'd come about the gutters. "That'd be it. Coastguard found it drifting and towed it back."

"Nobody told us they found it."

He considered that. "Well. Your mother, maybe."

Nell asked him what he remembered about that morning, and he told her about the weather, which had been fine, and the ferry, which had been late, and then, as she was leaving, he said: "I thought I saw him on it, you know. The ferry. Told the police and all. But I'd not got my glasses."

She walked back along the shingle in the dark. The house had one light on, the kitchen, where she'd left it. From out here it looked like someone was home.

She went into the shed again before bed and looked at the oars. Stowed. Somebody had stowed them.

## III

Her mother's wardrobe was the last thing, and she almost left it for the clearance men. But the bottom was false — their father had built it that way, for the good shoes — and under the shoes was a biscuit tin.

The letters were all the same size, in the same careful hand that leaned to the right. Postmarked Halifax, the first one dated four months after the search was called off. Nell read the top one standing up, and then she sat down on the floor of her mother's bedroom and read the rest, all forty-one, while the light went and came back.

*Don't tell Nell yet*, the first one said. *I'll tell her myself when I can.*

He never had. And neither, for nine years, had their mother.
