# Last Pick

Lise was in the Bramleys by six, before the dew burned off, because that was when her father had always started and she didn't know yet how to start any other way.

The funeral home had given her his things in a padded envelope: watch, wallet, reading glasses, keys. She'd gone through the keys on the drive back. House, truck, the cold store, the gate. And one small brass key she didn't know, which after some thought she understood must be the shed. Her father had kept the shed at the bottom of the orchard padlocked for thirty years, and in thirty years he had never once said why, and when she was eleven he had caught her trying the lock with a hairpin and not spoken to her for two days.

She put the key in her jeans pocket. She'd open it when she was ready.

By eight she had four crates and a sore back. The trees were heavy this year, which felt like a kind of insult.

---

Colm came up on Saturday with a folder.

"Harlan's offer is good," he said, laying it out on the kitchen table among the sympathy cards. "It's better than good. It clears Dad's overdraft and the loan on the cold store and leaves us each enough to actually do something."

"I'm doing something."

"You're picking apples on your own at six in the morning. That's not a plan, Lise, that's grief."

She looked at the folder, the architect's drawing of forty identical houses where the Coxes were now. "Mum planted the Bramleys."

"Mum planted the Bramleys in 1979. They're old trees. They're done."

"They're not done. Look at them."

"I'm not saying they don't have apples." He rubbed his face. "I'm saying you can't run this place alone, and we can't afford to pay anyone, and in two years the bank will sell it out from under you for less than Harlan's offering now."

She didn't answer. After a while he put the folder back in his bag and drove home.

---

Mr. Petrie had picked for her father every autumn since before she was born. He walked the rows with her on Tuesday, putting his hand on the trunks the way you'd check a horse's legs.

"Canker in this one," he said. "And this. You'll lose that whole end row in five years." He looked up into the Bramleys. "These have ten in them, maybe. Maybe."

"Would you stay on? Next year?"

He smiled at her kindly, which was worse than if he'd laughed. "I'm seventy-four, love. My knees are done. This is my last pick."

She thanked him and paid him cash from the tin, and when he'd gone she sat on an upturned crate and did the sums on her phone, and then did them again, and got the same answer both times.

---

Colm called on Thursday and asked her to meet him at the Plough.

"Harlan wants an answer by the end of the month," he said, once they had drinks. "The money's good, Lise. It clears the overdraft, it clears the loan, it leaves us each something."

"Mum planted those trees."

"I know she did. And they're old, and you can't run the place by yourself, and we can't pay anyone. If we wait, the bank does it for us."

"You keep saying that."

"Because it keeps being true."

She drank her cider and watched a man at the bar feed crisps to a dog. Colm waited, and when she didn't say anything else he finished his pint and said he had to get back for the kids.

---

The letter from Harlan Homes came the following Wednesday. The county had refused planning permission for the access road; without it the development wasn't viable; Harlan regretted that they were withdrawing their offer and wished the family well.

Lise read it twice standing at the gate. Then she walked down into the Bramleys, where the light was coming in low and gold between the rows, and stood there with the letter in her hand while the relief went through her like warmth coming back into cold fingers.

Colm rang that evening. "Well," he said. "Guess that's that, then."

"Guess so."

In the morning she was out by six. The windfalls were going soft in the grass, sweet and wasted, and the wasps had found them. She picked until her arms ached. It would be a good year, whatever came after.
