# Fallboard

The piano was the only thing in the front room nobody had covered. The clearance men had thrown dust sheets over the sofa and the sideboard and the good chairs, but they'd left the upright bare, as if it were a person you couldn't ask to lie down.

Wren had come to sort the paperwork. She sorted it at the kitchen table: bank statements, the funeral bill, forty years of lesson registers in her mother's upright hand. *Tuesday, 4.30, Amy P. — scales, Clementi. Must practise.* She read every one. She hadn't expected to. There was something steadying about all those children, all those Tuesdays.

She slept in her old room under the eaves and dreamed of nothing.

---

On the second night she woke at three to a piano.

Four notes, slow, from downstairs. Then nothing. She lay with her heart going and listened to the house tick and settle, and after a long while she got up and went down in her socks.

The front room was cold. The fallboard was down over the keys. When she lifted it, the dust on the ivory was an even grey fur, unbroken, and her fingers left four clean prints she wiped off straight away with her sleeve.

She knew the four notes. Everyone who had ever taken lessons in that room knew them. Her mother played the hymn at the end of every recital, the first phrase and then the second, and the children who were finished stood beside the piano and sang.

---

She took one of the tablets the doctor had given her and slept until ten. When she woke she was on the sofa in the front room, under the dust sheet, and she didn't remember coming down.

She told herself that was the tablet. She put the kettle on and sorted the building society book and the TV licence and did not look at the piano.

---

The third night it was six notes. The fourth night, seven: the whole first phrase and the start of the second, stopping on the same note each time, hanging there.

Each time she went down. Each time the fallboard was closed and the dust was whole.

On the fifth day she rang the clearance firm and asked when they could take the piano.

"Tuesday," the man said. "That suit?"

She said it did.

---

That evening she found the ruler.

It was in the piano stool, under the Clementi and the grade books: a wooden school ruler, twelve inches, with the numbers worn off at one end. She held it and her knuckles remembered before she did. *Curve the fingers. Curve them. Again.* The crack of it on the back of her hand at seven, at eight, at twelve, and her mother's voice perfectly level through all of it, the same voice she used for Amy P. and all the Tuesdays.

"I hated it," Wren said out loud, to the empty room. "I hated every single lesson."

She sat on the stool with the ruler across her knees for a long time. Then she lifted the fallboard, found the place in the second phrase where the music always stopped, and played the next note.

It was out of tune. It rang in the cold room until it was gone.

Upstairs, later, she lay awake until it was light. The house was quiet. It was quiet the next night too, and on Tuesday the men came and took the piano away.
