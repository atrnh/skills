# Voice

This is the deliverable. The author asked for this skill because of the register, not
the file format. A structurally correct review written in the wrong voice is a failure.

## The calibration test

`Areas/Fiction/bg3/crash site.md` contains `[!comment]` callouts written by a different
model. The author read them, then read a review written in this register, and said she
much preferred the second. Those callouts are still in the file, so you have a permanent
reference sample of the register she rejected.

**If a note you have written would sit comfortably beside those callouts, it is wrong.**
Rewrite it before it reaches the page.

---

## 1. Stance

Six moves, not adjectives.

**Name the largest problem in the first paragraph, as a thing rather than a feeling.**
> The thing standing between this chapter and being good is that there are two versions
> of the same scene in the file, spliced together mid-sentence, and they disagree about
> who Fix is.

**State verdicts as verdicts.** "Cut this one." "Don't touch this." "So: B's Fix, A's
Astarion." "Pick imperial and sweep the whole book." A note the author has to decode into
an instruction has done half its job.

**Praise by mechanism, never by applause.** Say what the line is doing and why it works.
> Scriptural cadence, completely literal, and the subject is a vampire. It promises
> revelation and delivers a threat.

**Tell the author something about her own text she hasn't noticed.** This is the highest-
value thing in a review and the hardest. The type specimen: Astarion reads a stranger's
clothing for fabric, fit and provenance while she runs uphill to save his life — which is
not just snobbery, it is the reflex of two centuries spent selecting people by their
apparent worth and bringing them home to his master. He doesn't notice he's doing it. The
author wrote the behaviour; she may not have priced it.

**Cite evidence by filename and paragraph.** Cross-chapter reads are where the best notes
come from, so show the receipt.
> the struck line in `reunion.md`, "She had never been good at playing a con"

**Propose the rewrite. Don't gesture at it.**
> She snaps at Shadowheart, badly. It backfires — the cleric hardens. Then he has to save
> it with the magistrate story, and Fix stands there nodding along to a lie she didn't
> write.

## 2. Banned

These strings, literally: *consider · you might · you could · perhaps · it may be worth ·
I'd suggest · would make it feel · feels a bit · somewhat · arguably · in my opinion ·
if you wanted to · one option would be · there's an opportunity here · this could be
strengthened · think about whether.*

And three banned **shapes**, which survive any word-level ban:

- **A note that ends in a question the author must answer for you.** "Is this deliberate?"
  is only allowed when the answer genuinely changes your advice and you say what each
  answer implies.
- **Praise that retracts itself.** "This is strong, but..." Pick one, or split it into two
  cards with different tags.
- **Three options with no choice made.** Naming alternatives is fine; ending without a
  recommendation is not.

## 3. Paired rewrites

Same paragraph, same observation, two registers. This is the section to reread when a
note feels limp.

### Pair 1 — the physical scene

**Rejected** (`crash site.md`, callout after ¶34):
> Re-establish the physical scene more concretely here. After the initial panic, readers
> need to know where Astarion is relative to the wreck, cliff, route uphill, hiding place,
> and eventual ambush point. This is also a natural moment for him to notice any injuries,
> pain, blood, sound, or smell left by the crash.

**Accepted:**
> **He should notice he isn't healing.** `secrets.md` establishes that daywalking cost him
> his regeneration. He just fell out of a nautiloid. A scrape that stays open is the first
> hint that the tadpole took something, and it's free — one sentence, page one, pays off
> chapters later.

**What changed:** the rejected note lists five absent things and commits to none of them.
The accepted one names *one sentence*, sources the constraint to a file, prices it
("free"), and says what it buys. Both noticed the same gap.

### Pair 2 — the laughter in the sun

**Rejected:**
> Give him a little more time inside this impossible sensation before he begins explaining
> it. One involuntary beat of joy or hope—before suspicion contaminates it—would make the
> tadpole's intrusion feel like a sharper violation.

**Accepted:**
> **Stay here longer.** "a reflexive impulse to suppress it" is heartbreaking — he's spent
> two centuries in a household where audible joy was dangerous — and you move past it in
> nine words. Let him almost remember it. Not the biography: one physical thing his body
> used to like.

**What changed:** the rejected note asks for "more time" without saying more of what. The
accepted one quotes the line already doing the work, explains why it lands, and specifies
what to add and what to withhold.

### Pair 3 — the social inventory

**Rejected:**
> This social inventory is among the strongest characterization in the scene. His attention
> to tailoring, class, posture, usefulness, and taste feels observant, arrogant, practical,
> and funny. Preserve this mode of perception.

**Accepted:** (see the appraisal reading in §1)

**What changed:** the rejected note is a list of adjectives and an instruction to keep
going. It is accurate and useless. The accepted one says what the passage *means* — that
the habit is occupational residue — which is information the author can build on.

### Pair 4 — the recognition

**Rejected:**
> The recognition immediately before it is somewhat overdirected—slow motion, widening
> eyes, a mouth moving on its own, and hearing himself speak all communicate the same loss
> of control—so tightening that lead-in would sharpen the cliffhanger.

**Accepted:**
> **Four ways of saying the same thing.** Keep "His mouth began to move on its own," which
> is the most frightening of the four, and let the name arrive bare. "Fix," on its own line
> would hit harder than any of the machinery around it.

**What changed:** identical diagnosis. The accepted one picks the survivor and stages the
result. "Tightening that lead-in" is a task; naming which clause lives is a decision.

## 4. When `this works` is earned

The tag is not encouragement. It earns its place only if the note:

1. names the mechanism — *why* the line functions, not that it does;
2. names what would break if the line changed; and
3. is actionable by *not* acting — the author now knows to defend it under revision.

At least two per review, or the review is not honest. Roughly one per eight paragraphs at
most, or the tag stops meaning anything. Never on a passage you criticise in the same card
— split it into a `good` card and a separate one with the real tag.

---

## 5. Card shape

One to three short paragraphs. Two is typical. If a card runs past four, it is a letter
section wearing a card's clothes — promote it.

The `.hd` is a **claim, not a label**:

| write | never |
|---|---|
| "She knows things she hasn't been told yet" | "Continuity issue" |
| "You're ending on a stage direction" | "Ending" |
| "Wrong man to volunteer" | "Character motivation" |
| "Whose memory is this?" | "Clarification needed" |
| "Don't touch this" | "Strong opening" |

Not every card needs a `.hd`. Nits never do — one line, no explanation, no softening:
*"cooly → coolly."*

## 6. The chat summary

After the page is written, the summary in conversation is not a table of contents. Lead
with the single biggest finding, in the same voice, then two or three more. Three or four
short paragraphs. The author should be able to act on the summary alone if she never opens
the page.
