# Analysis passes

Run these in order. Line-level notes written before the structural passes are usually
notes on prose that should not survive revision.

---

## Pass 1 — Is this one draft?

The most common and most valuable finding in this vault, and it invalidates most other
notes until it is settled. Symptoms:

- The same beat firing twice — a blush discovered twice, a line of dialogue delivered
  twice, a character introduced who was already introduced.
- A quotation that stops mid-sentence. This is almost always the seam.
- An argument someone concedes, re-escalates, then concedes again.
- Two stretches that characterise the same person in opposite directions.
- A scene that resolves, then resolves again differently.

When you find one:

1. **Locate the seam** and give its paragraph number.
2. **Say which side you would keep** — never present both and let the author choose.
3. **Justify by what the rest of the book needs**, not by which prose is prettier. In
   `tadpoles.md` the two passes split as *better plot* (the cover story that `reunion.md`
   and `secrets.md` both depend on) versus *better character* (a protagonist her own book
   says is bad at cons). The answer was to take the character from one and the plot from
   the other, not to pick a winner.
4. **List every doubled beat** so the author can strip the survivors.

Note that a draft may be mid-surgery. `%%…%%` comments can hide an entire alternate
version — check the excluded blocks from `extract` before concluding a scene is missing.

---

## Pass 2 — Is the best material spent well?

Find the best sentence in the chapter. Then check whether anything earlier has already
cashed it.

A reveal that arrives as trivia has been pre-spent. In `crash site.md` the daywalking
reveal — the fact the entire AU turns on — lands inside a paragraph about the eight planets
of Realmspace. In `tadpoles.md` the vampire-in-daylight line sits at ¶35, after two earlier
paragraphs have already leaked his criminal history and his master.

Ask: what is the single most surprising fact in this chapter, and does the reader receive
it at full strength or at a discount?

---

## Pass 3 — What does the rest of the book need from this chapter?

This is what the extra reads are for, and it is where the notes the author cannot write
herself come from.

- **A lie or cover story told here that later chapters assume landed.** If chapters four
  and six are built on it, it has to be told well on the page, not glossed.
- **A cost or rule established later that should have been planted here.** `secrets.md`
  says daywalking cost Astarion his regenerative healing — so chapter one owes the reader
  a scrape that won't close.
- **A thesis stated and never paid.** `crash site.md` ¶6 makes the character's deepest fear
  erasure — no corpse, no mourner, no proof he existed — and then he survives and thinks
  about plasma. Setups stronger than their payoffs are a reliable find.
- **Something conspicuously absent.** A vampire's POV chapter that never mentions hunger.
  A newly-freed spawn who never once thinks about his master. Absences are hard to see
  because nothing on the page points at them; check the character's known appetites and
  fears against what the chapter actually contains.

---

## Pass 4 — Continuity ledger

Cheap to run, and the findings are unarguable. Check:

- **Knowledge before acquisition.** Does the POV character use names, titles or facts they
  have not been told yet? Introductions staged twice are common.
- **Unclaimed titles.** "archmage" for a man who has only said "I'm Gale."
- **Units and anachronism.** This vault mixes metric and imperial (*a kilometer* on the
  Sword Coast, *two meters of grave dirt*) and occasionally reaches for words that don't
  exist in the setting (*neon*).
- **Who triggers what.** Adjacent chapters claiming the same causal moment.
- **Elastic distance.** Characters overhearing full sentences from people described as heat
  mirages, then reading fabric weave off them.
- **Struck-through text anywhere in the folder.** A line the author cut is testimony about
  the character and outranks how that character behaves in any single scene.

---

## Pass 5 — Prose and typos

Only now. Patterns worth naming rather than listing instance by instance:

- **Metaphor stacking** — a second and third vehicle recruited because the first felt
  insufficient. Name the cause, not just the symptom.
- **Repeated verdicts** — four adjectives on one act and no concrete consequence.
- **Register collisions** — elevated diction inside an adrenaline sentence.
- **Overdirection** — four simultaneous signals of the same loss of control.
- **Species/world glosses mid-moment** — a character explaining their own kind to
  themselves.
- **Physical tics** — the same gesture as the character's default reaction across chapters.

Typos go last, as one-liners, in the letter and as `nit` cards. Do not spread a
straight-vs-curly apostrophe problem across nine cards; say it once and say where.

---

## What a finished review contains

- A letter whose first paragraph names the best thing and whose second names the biggest
  problem, flatly.
- Two to five themed sections, titled as claims.
- A numbered rebuild sequence the author can work straight through.
- A continuity ledger.
- Typos.
- Paragraph cards where there is something to say, and empty comment columns where there
  isn't. Roughly a third of rows carrying no note is normal and correct.
