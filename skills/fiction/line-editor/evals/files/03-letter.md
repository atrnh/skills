Owen found the letter on the third day, folded inside his mother's copy of the seed catalog, between the pole beans and the peas. He knew her handwriting before he knew what it said: the looped d's, the way she crossed her t's high, as if she meant to come back for them.

It was addressed to him. It was dated the spring he left for Tulsa.

He didn't open it. He set it on the kitchen table, squared it to the edge, and went outside to finish the gutters. The ladder ticked against the siding as he climbed. From the roof he could see the whole valley, the river flat and white under the overcast, the Pruitts' cattle standing in the low field as though someone had placed them there and forgotten.

When he came down it was nearly dark. The letter was where he'd left it. He made coffee he didn't want and drank half of it standing up. Then he sat, and turned the envelope over, and saw that it had already been opened, carefully, along the top, and sealed again with a strip of her yellow tape.
