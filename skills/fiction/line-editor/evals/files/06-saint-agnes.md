## Ivy

Grandpa drives like he's taking a test. Both hands on the wheel, ten and two, forty in a fifty-five, and every time a truck passes he says *well* like it's a whole sentence. I have my earbuds in but nothing's playing. I just don't want him to talk about Gran, because if he talks about Gran he'll do the voice, the calm one, the one he used when the dog died, and I literally cannot.

The parking garage at Saint Agnes is like a level in a game nobody beats. Level three, row F. He writes it on his hand with a pen from the glove box, which, okay. Smart.

Inside, everything smells like hand sanitizer and soup. The lady at the desk gives us name stickers like it's a field trip. Grandpa stuck his to his coat, perfectly straight, and pressed it flat with his thumb.

Room 412. The curtain's half open. I can see the shape of Gran's feet under the blanket, and I'm like, okay, those are her feet, they're just feet, and then I don't go in.

## Walter

I had not driven to Saint Agnes since the winter Margaret had her gallbladder out, and I found that the city had moved its exits in the interval, as cities will, without consulting the people who must use them. Ivy sat beside me with those wires in her ears and said nothing for forty minutes, which I took as a kindness, although I suspect it was not intended as one.

At the desk a young woman printed our names onto stickers. I wore mine as instructed. I have always found hospitals easier to bear if I do what I am told; it gives the hands an occupation while the mind is elsewhere.

The nurse on the fourth floor was a heavyset man with a gentle voice, and when I asked after my wife he was like, she's resting, go on in. I thanked him. I did not go in at once. I stood at the curtain beside my granddaughter and looked at Margaret's feet beneath the blanket, and I could not for the life of me remember whether she had always slept with them crossed.
