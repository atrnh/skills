# Curriculum rubric

Version: 2

This rubric judges whether curriculum helps its learners understand and do what it sets out to
teach. It covers lectures, labs, exercises, homework, assessments, content outlines, and whole
courses.

A gate criterion with a median below 7 means the material isn't ready, however well it scores
elsewhere.

## Scope

- **A lecture, lab, exercise, homework assignment, or assessment.** Read all of it, along with
  anything learners need from it, such as starter code, data files, or linked setup pages.
  Score every criterion that applies.
- **A content outline.** Judge the plan as the material it describes. Score accuracy,
  completeness of coverage, audience fit, objectives, progression, and the planned examples and
  practice. Language for purpose and presented form are usually `"na"`.
- **A whole course.** Read the course outline and index, then the units named for in-depth
  reading in the review's scope, and skim the rest. Judge progression and alignment across
  units as well as within them, and name the unit in every quote and finding.

When the review's context places the material in a course, judge it as part of that sequence:
what learners have already been taught, what this item is meant to accomplish, and what it
prepares them for. Files listed as context aren't under review. Cite them only to show that the
material doesn't match them. When the context says no course structure was found, judge the
material on its own.

## How to read

Read the material once in order as the learner described in the review's context, at their
pace. Note where you slow down, get lost, would need to search elsewhere, or stop trusting the
material. Then score each criterion, returning to the text for evidence.

## Where a problem belongs

Score each problem under the criterion that owns it. Lower another criterion only when the
problem separately harms what that criterion measures.

- Support learners need that the material doesn't supply belongs to **completeness**.
- Support that's supplied but pitched at the wrong level belongs to **audience fit**.
- Support that's supplied but arrives after learners need it belongs to **progression**.
- A wrong fact or a step that doesn't work belongs to **accuracy**, even when it also leaves a
  gap.

## Criteria

### AC Accuracy (gate)

Facts, terminology, code, commands, interface paths, and claims are correct and current for the
tools and versions the material names.

- **Strong (9–10):** You found no errors. Code and commands would work as written, and claims
  are precise.
- **Sound (7–8):** Minor slips that a learner would recover from without being misled, such as
  a typo in a name that doesn't matter to any step.
- **Weak (4–6):** Errors learners can recover from, but only with effort or outside help, such
  as a step that fails until they work out the fix, or a misleading claim on a secondary point.
- **Failing (1–3):** Errors that prevent the intended outcome or teach a core concept wrongly,
  such as a required step that can't succeed as written.

If you can't run the code, judge it by reading and name what you couldn't verify in the
rationale. Answer `"cannot_assess"` only when accuracy depends entirely on something you can't
check.

### CO Completeness (gate)

Learners can understand and do what's asked without knowledge, setup, or resources that the
material neither supplies nor states as a prerequisite. Something taught earlier in the course,
according to the review's context, counts as supplied.

- **Strong (9–10):** Every step, term, prerequisite, and resource learners need is supplied or
  stated.
- **Sound (7–8):** Small gaps that learners at this level would fill without help.
- **Weak (4–6):** A gap that would stall many learners or send them to search elsewhere.
- **Failing (1–3):** Learners can't follow or complete the material as written.

### AU Audience fit

The level, assumed knowledge, pace, and tone suit the stated learners. The tone is friendly and
mentoring without talking down.

- **Strong (9–10):** Pitched for the stated learners throughout. New terms are defined where
  they first appear, and the tone encourages without padding.
- **Sound (7–8):** Mostly right, with a few passages that are too advanced, too basic, or off in
  tone.
- **Weak (4–6):** Repeatedly assumes knowledge the learners lack or labors what they already
  know, or uses a tone that would discourage them.
- **Failing (1–3):** Written for a different audience.

### OB Objectives and alignment

The material states or clearly implies what learners will be able to do afterward, and its
explanations, practice, and checks of understanding all serve those goals. When the course
outline states objectives for this item, judge against those.

- **Strong (9–10):** The goals are clear, every section and activity serves them, and
  assessment items measure the goals rather than trivia.
- **Sound (7–8):** The goals are clear, but one or two parts drift from them, or an assessment
  item tests something peripheral.
- **Weak (4–6):** The goals are vague or missing, substantial content or practice serves none of
  them, or the assessment doesn't measure them.
- **Failing (1–3):** No goal can be discerned, or the material works against its stated goal.

### PR Progression

Each part has a reason to follow the one before it and a purpose in the work ahead. In a
course, the same holds for the item as a whole: it builds on what came before it and prepares
learners for what follows.

- **Strong (9–10):** Each idea's prerequisites come before it. Transitions state how parts
  relate, and questions that would block understanding are answered before they do.
- **Sound (7–8):** A sound order with a weak transition, or a question left open longer than it
  should be.
- **Weak (4–6):** Learners must use ideas before they're taught, or related topics follow one
  another with no logic a learner could follow.
- **Failing (1–3):** The order prevents understanding.

A question left open on purpose, when resolving it is part of the learning, isn't a flaw.

### EX Examples and concepts

Examples, case studies, sample code, and demonstrations help learners recognize and use an idea
beyond the example itself.

- **Strong (9–10):** Each example has a stated purpose. Learners get the grounding to interpret
  it first, its details are tied to the concept and its terminology, and the reasoning transfers
  to other situations. Demonstrations stay focused on the cause and result they show.
- **Sound (7–8):** The examples work, but one lacks an explicit connection to its concept, or a
  demonstration runs longer than its teaching value.
- **Weak (4–6):** Learners could copy the examples without understanding why they work, or
  concepts that need an example have none.
- **Failing (1–3):** The examples mislead, or have no evident connection to the concepts.

An example can come before its concept when it gives learners a question or something to
investigate. Judge whether it supplies enough context for that purpose and is connected to the
concept afterward. Answer `"na"` only when the material's purpose calls for no examples.

### LA Language for purpose

Each passage's language matches its teaching purpose: explaining a concept, narrating a
demonstration, or directing practice.

- **Strong (9–10):** Explanations say what happens, why it matters, and how ideas connect.
  Demonstrations tie each step to its purpose and result. Practice instructions make the next
  action unmistakable.
- **Sound (7–8):** An occasional mismatch, such as an explanation that only lists steps.
- **Weak (4–6):** Frequent mismatches, such as instructions buried in explanation, or concepts
  delivered as commands with no reasoning.
- **Failing (1–3):** Learners can't tell what they're meant to understand or what they're meant
  to do.

### PD Practice design

Exercises, labs, and other practice spend learners' effort on the skill they're meant to
develop. Answer `"na"` when the material has no practice and its purpose calls for none. When it
needs practice and has none, score it low.

- **Strong (9–10):** Learners make the decisions the skill requires, with starting points
  supplied where inventing them would be incidental work. The expected outcome and deliverable
  are concrete, and the intended challenge is intact.
- **Sound (7–8):** Mostly well designed, with some incidental work or a somewhat vague
  deliverable.
- **Weak (4–6):** Learners spend significant time on incidental work, would have to guess what
  counts as success, or are handed the reasoning they were meant to practice.
- **Failing (1–3):** The activity doesn't practice the intended skill, or can't be completed in
  the time available.

### PF Presented form

In slides, handouts, presenter notes, or another presented form, learners can follow the
reasoning at the point where they meet each idea. Answer `"na"` when there is no presented form.

- **Strong (9–10):** Each idea's premises appear before it in the form learners see. Related
  ideas stay together, breaks fall at meaningful shifts, and notes add depth without holding the
  essential connection.
- **Sound (7–8):** Mostly followable, with one awkward split or one connection that appears only
  in notes.
- **Weak (4–6):** Essential reasoning lives only in the source or the notes, or slides present
  conclusions without their premises.
- **Failing (1–3):** Learners can't follow the presented form.

When the review supplies rendered slides or pages, judge from them. When you have only the
source, judge from it if it shows where each slide or page breaks and which text is notes, and
say so in the rationale. If it doesn't show that, answer `"cannot_assess"`.

## For the chair

Everything above this heading goes to reviewers. This section is for the chair alone.

### Place the material in its course

Every item smaller than a whole course, including a content outline for one lecture or lab, is
judged as part of its sequence. Find that sequence before briefing the reviewers.

1. **Find the course structure.** Check the project's own instructions, such as `AGENTS.md` or
   its README, for where course outlines and indexes live. Then look in the material's folder
   and the folders above it, up to the project root, for a course outline, index, table of
   contents, syllabus, or a README that lists the course's parts.
2. **Read only what placement needs.** Read the course outline or index, the items immediately
   before and after this one, and anything the material explicitly refers to. Don't read the
   whole course.
3. **Write the placement as facts with sources.** Every reviewer relies on it, so a misreading
   here misleads the whole panel. Record titles, the outline's stated objectives, and what
   neighboring items cover according to their headings and objectives, not your judgment of
   them. Give each source's absolute path so reviewers can check it.
4. **Report what doesn't line up.** When the outline and the actual files disagree, as when the
   outline promises a topic the material doesn't cover, record both and don't choose between
   them.
5. **Say when nothing was found.** Reviewers then judge the material on its own. Ask the user
   first when the material clearly belongs to a course, such as one numbered "Lab 2.3" or kept
   in a course folder, and the placement would change the scores.

Add the placement to `context.md`:

```markdown
## Place in the course

- **Course:** <title>, for <audience>. Outline: </absolute/path>
- **This item:** <unit and number>, "<title>". The outline's objectives for it: <objectives>
- **Before it:** <number>, "<title>" (</absolute/path>). Covers: <topics from its headings>
- **After it:** <number>, "<title>" (</absolute/path>). Expects learners to: <objectives>
- **Mismatches:** <where the outline and the files disagree, or "None found">
```

List every placement source in `sources.json` under `context`, so reviewers can cite them.

### Provide the presented form

When the material has slides, handouts, or another presented form, reviewers judge presented
form best from what learners actually see. If the project can render it, such as with a slide
build or a PDF export, add the rendered pages or slide images to the context files. Otherwise,
say in `context.md` that reviewers have only the source. Converted text, as from a PowerPoint
file, should keep each slide's boundary and keep notes separate from what's on the slide.

### Maintain this rubric

Keep the criteria table's format, because `scripts/aggregate.py` reads codes and gates from
it. Raise the version whenever a change could move scores, so that an improvement loop never
compares runs scored against different rubrics.
