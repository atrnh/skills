# Curriculum rubric: lecture module

Version: 1

This review is of a lecture that an instructor delivers from slides or another presented form,
so four more criteria apply. Score them after the criteria above, in this order. None is a gate.

## Where a problem belongs in a lecture

These rules add to the ones above.

- Whether the lecture fits its time slot, and how quickly new ideas arrive, belongs to **pacing
  and load**. Audience fit keeps the level, assumed knowledge, and tone.
- Whether learners are asked to think at intervals, whether a question comes before its answer,
  and whether a check would reveal a misunderstanding belong to **engagement and checks**.
  Practice design keeps whether the lecture's activities practice the skill its goals name.
- Guidance for the instructor belongs to **teachability**. Reasoning that learners need but
  that appears only in the notes belongs to **presented form**.

## PF Presented form

In slides, handouts, presenter notes, or another presented form, learners can follow the
reasoning at the point where they meet each idea.

- **Strong (9–10):** Each idea's premises appear before it in the form learners see. Related
  ideas stay together, breaks fall at meaningful shifts, and notes add depth without holding the
  essential connection. Slides carry nothing that competes with their point, and words sit
  beside the picture or code they describe.
- **Sound (7–8):** Mostly followable, with one awkward split or one connection that appears only
  in notes.
- **Weak (4–6):** Essential reasoning lives only in the source or the notes, slides present
  conclusions without their premises, or slides are crowded enough to bury their point.
- **Failing (1–3):** Learners can't follow the presented form.

When the review supplies rendered slides or pages, judge from them. When you have only the
source, judge from it if it shows where each slide or page breaks and which text is notes, and
say so in the rationale. If it doesn't show that, answer `cannot_assess`.

## PC Pacing and load

The lecture fits its time slot, new ideas arrive at a rate learners can absorb, and dense
stretches are broken up by examples, activities, or recaps.

- **Strong (9–10):** The material fits the slot with room for the activities and questions it
  plans. Each new idea has time to land before the next one depends on it.
- **Sound (7–8):** It fits, but one stretch is crowded, or the slot is tight enough that a
  planned activity or question would likely be cut.
- **Weak (4–6):** Clearly more than the slot holds, so the instructor must rush or cut, or
  several new ideas arrive in a row with no example, activity, or pause between them.
- **Failing (1–3):** It couldn't be delivered in its slot, or it's too dense for learners to
  follow live.

Estimate the time from what's there, such as slides, activities, and demos, and show the
estimate in the rationale. When the review's context gives no time slot, judge only how quickly
new ideas arrive, and say so.

## EN Engagement and checks

At intervals, learners do something with the ideas: recall, predict, answer, or decide. Each
question comes before its answer, and each check would show learners and the instructor whether
the idea landed.

- **Strong (9–10):** Learners are asked to think at regular points, including recalling earlier
  material before it's built on. Every question appears before its answer. Checks are specific
  enough that a wrong answer shows what was misunderstood.
- **Sound (7–8):** Regular checks, but one long stretch has none, or a check is too general to
  reveal anything, such as "Any questions?"
- **Weak (4–6):** Checks are rare or only rhetorical, answers appear with their questions so
  learners can't attempt them first, or the checks couldn't reveal a misunderstanding.
- **Failing (1–3):** Learners only listen. Nothing asks them to think with the ideas.

Judge whether a question comes before its answer from where the slide breaks and reveals fall.

## TE Teachability

An instructor who didn't write the lecture could teach it from the material. The notes say what
to stress and what to ask, the answers to expect, including likely wrong ones and how to respond,
roughly how long each part takes, and what to do when a live demo fails.

- **Strong (9–10):** Notes give each section's point, the questions to ask with expected and
  likely wrong answers, timing cues, and a fallback for each live demo.
- **Sound (7–8):** Notes cover the main points and questions but miss expected answers, timing,
  or a demo fallback.
- **Weak (4–6):** Notes mostly repeat the slides, or leave the instructor to work out what an
  activity is for or how to run it.
- **Failing (1–3):** No notes, and the slides alone don't show what to teach or how.

## For the chair

Everything above this heading goes to reviewers, after the core's reviewer text. This section is
for the chair alone.

### Provide the presented form

Reviewers judge presented form best from what learners actually see. If the project can render
it, such as with a slide build or a PDF export, list the rendered pages or slide images in
`context.md` among the files under review, beside the text they render, and name each image so
reviewers can match it to its slide or page in the text. Otherwise, say in `context.md` that
reviewers have only the source. Converted text, as from a PowerPoint file, should keep each
slide's boundary and keep notes separate from what's on the slide.

### Give the time slot

Pacing and load is judged against the lecture's time slot. Record it in the placement with its
source, such as the course outline, syllabus, or user. Without it, reviewers can judge only how
quickly new ideas arrive.
